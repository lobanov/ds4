## Step-by-Step Explanation of Gradient Descent

**Goal:** Minimize a loss function \( J(\theta) \) by iteratively adjusting parameters \( \theta \) in the direction that reduces the loss most quickly.

### Step 1: Define the Loss Function
Choose a differentiable function that measures error (e.g., mean squared error for regression, cross-entropy for classification). The parameters \( \theta \) (e.g., weights and biases) are what we want to optimize.

### Step 2: Compute the Gradient
Calculate the derivative (or partial derivatives) of the loss function with respect to each parameter. The gradient \( \nabla J(\theta) \) points in the direction of steepest *increase* of the loss.

### Step 3: Move Opposite to the Gradient
Since we want to *minimize* the loss, we update parameters in the opposite direction of the gradient:
\[
\theta_{\text{new}} = \theta_{\text{old}} - \alpha \cdot \nabla J(\theta_{\text{old}})
\]
Here \( \alpha \) is the **learning rate** – a small positive number that controls step size.

### Step 4: Repeat
Recompute the gradient at the new parameter values, then update again. Continue until:
- The gradient becomes very close to zero (minimum reached), or
- A maximum number of iterations is reached, or
- The loss stops decreasing significantly.

### Step 5: Convergence
At the optimum, the gradient is zero (or near zero). The algorithm may oscillate around the minimum if the learning rate is too large, or converge slowly if too small.

## Example 1: Minimizing a Simple Quadratic Function

**Problem:** Find the minimum of \( f(x) = x^2 + 2 \).  
We know the answer is \( x = 0 \), but let's use gradient descent.

- **Loss:** \( J(x) = x^2 + 2 \)  
- **Gradient:** \( \frac{dJ}{dx} = 2x \)  
- **Initial guess:** \( x = 3 \)  
- **Learning rate:** \( \alpha = 0.1 \)

**Iteration 1:**  
Gradient = \( 2 \times 3 = 6 \)
